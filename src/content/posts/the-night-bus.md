The night bus is a lit room that moves. Inside it, everyone is practising being alone in public: the nurse with her shoes in a bag, the boy rehearsing an apology into his collar, the man asleep against the window with the whole city sliding across his face like a slow film he is not watching. The driver knows us only by the doors we choose.

I sit near the back, where the engine hums up through the seat like a cat. At every stop the doors fold open and the cold climbs in to look around, and every time it decides not to stay. We pass the bakery where they are already awake, men in white aprons dusted like moths, sliding trays into the mouth of the oven. Somewhere a dog is barking at the idea of us.

I have taken this bus so many times that it has started to take me. When my stop comes I almost miss it. I almost let it carry me past the end of the line, to the depot where the buses sleep in rows, their lights off, their windows full of nothing, dreaming whatever buses dream: of a route with no stops, of a passenger who never gets off, of a morning that comes and asks them nothing at all.
