Every poem I have finished was finished by subtraction. Not at the end, as a tidying, but all the way through: a word goes in, and then, a day or a year later, it comes out, and the poem is suddenly able to breathe in a way it could not before. I used to call this editing. Now I think it is closer to listening.

There is a kind of silence that only exists because something was said around it. A rest in music is not the absence of music; it is music that has decided, for a moment, to be quiet. The white space at the end of a line of poetry works the same way. When a line breaks, the eye has to travel back across the page, and in that small journey something happens: a breath, a doubt, a turn. The line break is a door left open. You can stand in it.

* * *

I keep a notebook of words I have taken out of poems. It is not a sad book. It is more like a drawer of buttons, each one saved from a coat I no longer wear, each one still perfectly good. *Luminous* is in there, more than once. So is *ache*. So is *somehow*, which I put into almost every first draft the way some people salt water before it boils: out of habit, not need.

What surprises me is how often the missing word is still in the poem, invisibly. A reader feels its shape. If you write *the kettle* and not *the old kettle*, the oldness does not disappear; it moves into the rest of the poem, into the stairs that creak and the door that sticks. The adjective you deleted becomes the weather of the whole house.

* * *

There is a version of this that hardens into superstition, a cult of less. I do not believe a poem is better simply because it is shorter, or that the blank page is the only perfect poem. A poem can be generous, even crowded, the way a kitchen table can be crowded with people who love each other. The question is never *how many words* but *which ones are doing the work*, and which are only standing near the work, holding their coats.

When I revise now, I read the poem aloud and listen for the places where my voice wants to hurry. Hurrying usually means a word is in the way. Then I read it again and listen for the places where my voice wants to stop, and I ask whether the page is letting it. Sometimes the answer is a line break. Sometimes it is a stanza break, a longer silence, a room between rooms. Sometimes it is a word removed, and the sentence closes over the gap like water.

* * *

I think this is why I love the margin. It is the part of the page that belongs to no one yet. The poem lives in the middle and the margins hold their breath around it. In old books you find the margins full of other people: a pencilled question, a date, a line underlined twice by someone who needed it on a particular day. Those marks are a kind of answer. The poem left room, and someone stepped into it.

So: leave a word out. Leave it out and see what grows in the space. If nothing does, put it back; no harm done, the drawer is full of buttons. But if something does, if the line suddenly lifts, if the silence starts to mean, then you have learned something no amount of adding could teach you. What we do not say is part of what we say. The white of the page is not empty. It is listening.
